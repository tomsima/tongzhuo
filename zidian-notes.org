* 童
** etymology
An ancient character. Originally meant /male criminal/, which developed into /boy servant/, then later expanded to /infant, child/. In literary texts the meaning is extended even further from /infant/ to /bald/ (as infants are associated with sparse hair). The original meaning of /male criminal/ is shown in the depiction of 辛 _xīn_ /a torture tool > punishment/ at the top (now simplified to 立), being used to poke out an eye (a common punishment for prisoners of war), with the phonetic 東 _dōng_ at the bottom (now simplified to 里).

* 女
** etymology
The Shuōwén Seal form of 女 derives from old pictographs sketching a woman; in much older graphs the woman seems to be kneeling/sitting; a central loop represents the folded arms (as cradling a child), or breasts; the first stroke (㇛) of the modern form corresponds to the central curved stroke of the Seal form.
Compare: 母 _mǔ_ /mother/, 毋 _wú_ /do not/don't/; these are related modifications of 女, and help clarify its structure: in 母 _mǔ_ /mother/ two dots are added to mark the nipples/breasts (of a nursing mother); and in 毋 _wú_ /do not/ the two dots are a single line (抹胸 old-style garment covering/binding the breasts, chest-wrap/halter/bodice/vest/brassière; the strokes of 母 (omitting the two dots) can be seen as a variant of 女.
Ancient graphs associated with 女/母/毋 are very similar to each other; 坤(申) _kūn_ /female/, 身 _shēn_ /(pregnant) body/, 中 _zhōng_ /center/
Some commentators suggest that the 'kneeling woman' is an indication of the subservient position of women in traditional Chinese society, but this is almost certainly an ethnodeviant reading of the meaning (ie. it simply doesn't have any connotation relating to social position).

* 虎
** etymology
Derives from very old pictographs depicting the animal with feet/claws to the left, head and gaping jaws above, and long tail below.
In later ages the character became stylized and its interpretation became less clear; the lower part of 虎 (like 几/儿 in the modern forms) suggests a tiger rearing up on its hind legs (standing up like a man), or a man-eating tiger, but formerly represented the legs of a deer killed by the tiger.
Gradually two stylised forms were popularised, one with 几 at the bottom and one with 巾 representing a 'tiger skin pelt'. The latter became the basis for the cursive form, while the former became the basis for the running and modern regular forms

* 我
** etymology
originally meant /serrated axe/. At some very early stage it came to mean 'I, me', presumably a rebus phonetic borrowing: the word _wǒ_ /I, me/ existed in the spoken language; when people needed to write this down, they drew a picture of the _wǒ_, a well-known battle axe, so when read aloud it would sound the same.
there is a rare variant of this character where the original appearance can still be seen: 𢦐
the cursive form of this character was invented at a very early date, and differs noticeably from the running script. It appears to treat the character as 禾, putting the dot from 戈 above the 禾and then writing the 𫠠. The reason for this difference is probably because at the time the scribes were first writing in cursive, the wo weapon was still in common use, and they likely saw the character as the head of the weapon 彐 + the handle 𫠠 and so wrote in that order. The running script goes through the character according to the later abstracted version, resulting in a very different running cursive form.

* 异
** etymology
The modern simple form 异 _yì_ /different/ *conflates* two separate characters:异 and 異. Defrancis says异 has long been used as an  abbreviation of 異 _yì_ /different/, but I'm not so sure. Note that the oldest usages of these two characters were different:
1. The Shuōwén Seal form of 异(𢍀) _yì_ /raise/lift up (hands/etc., in surprise/etc.); use (hands/etc.)/ combines 巳(㠯已以) _yǐ_ /use/ (phonosemantic), and 廾(𠬞) _gǒng_ /upraised hands/; the component 廾 was added to 巳 in order to distinguish certain old meanings of 巳/以 /use/usual/ to give the meaning /special use/unusual/
2. The Shuōwén Seal form of 異(𠔱) _yì_ /divide/distinguish (differences)/ combines 廾(𠬞艸) _gǒng_ /raised hands/, and 畀(畁) _bì_ /give/; 廾/艸 is inserted in the middle of 畀, making a distinction between giving and receiving (between this/mine and that/yours); the modern form 異 looks like 田 over 共, but these are not the original components.

Old oracle bone and seal graphs associated with 異 are said to be old forms of 戴 _dài_ /put/wear (something on the head)/, perhaps a dramatic/ritual mask; other old forms are said to depict a person with upraised hands shielding/protecting the face (in the face of danger/attack). This character then had 𢦏 appended, perhaps as the meaning became conflated with 載. In SW the meanings of 戴 are said to extend from those of 異 /different/ (a person (actor) putting on (wearing) a mask (hat/glasses/etc.) becomes/seems different). 

Compare: 戴, 翼, 冀, 瀵, 粪/糞, in which 異 is a component, but not simplified to 异.

The development of the abstract meaning: 既(旣) _jì_ /finish a small meal/ > /finished/ > /already/, combines 皀 _jí_ /fragrant grain/ (phonosemantic), and 旡 _jì_ /belch (after eating)/ (phonosemantic).
* 贺
Originally meant /to present celebratory/congratulatory gifts/, hence 加 (phonosemantic) /increase/ + 贝 /money/
Compare with 嘉 _jiā_ /praise/honor/ 
* 凶
凶 and 兇 were originally different characters with different (but replated) meanings, which have been *conflated* in the simplified:

1. 凶 /terrible, disaster/
2. 兇 /violent, cruel (person)/

凶 is said to depict a 凵 _qiǎn_ /gaping pit/ (pitfall, spiked trap) into which 乂 someone/something has fallen or may fall. Adding 儿 makes this idea of 'terrible' into a person who is terrible
* 灭
** etymology
灭 is an invention from the first round of simplification. It takes the inner bottom left part of the original character as its full form. Its not a bad simplification imo.
滅 is 氵 /water/ + 烕 /extinguish, destroy/; 烕 is 火 /fire/ + 戌 /destroy/
* 且
** etymology
且  originally depicted a /sacrificial altar/: a 几 /table/stand/altar/ with 二 two shelves, standing on the 一 ground; the old form 𠀃 lacked the shelves.
Older graphs associated with 且  are connected with 祖 _zǔ_ /ancestor; ancestral tablet/, and esp. with 俎 _zǔ_ /sacrificial/festal vessel/.
* 召
** etymology
combines 刀 _dāo_ /knife/ (phonosemantic)  and 口 /mouth/voice/: a call to 刀 arms (征召), a call for support (招致).
* 刀
** etymology
a depiction of a knife. The perpendicular stroke appears to be a remnant of the crossguard (sits between the hilt and the blade), or the bolster (the thick bottom of the blade). The blade at some point changed to be drawn with just a single stroke, presumably because at some point /everyone/ was familiar with this character and it no longer needed to be drawn in full. The abstraction appears to have taken place very early in the seal script era, and the character was stable from then on.
* 邕
** etymology
combines 川(巛) _chuān_ /river/, and 邑 _yì_ /city-state/; the moat was a river channel engineered to ring the city, to defend it and carry away sewage/refuse; the old variant form 𡿷 is reminiscent of 营(營) /camp/; cf. 宫(宮) _gōng_ /palace/.

* 真
** etymology
Outlier: originally 貝 semantic (for the original meaning /valuable/), 十 (𠤎)  is a corruption of a lost sound component, 一 + 八 at the bottom are a corruption of 丁, also a sound compononent (the sound of 𠤎 was forgotten, so 丁 was added, but now that has been forgotten also!)
Defrancis etymology is rather apocryphal: 真(眞) _zhēn_ originally meant /transcendent Daoist immortal/. The seal form combines:
- 𠤎 _huà_ /die/ (man upside down)
- 目 _mù_ /eyes/vision/form/
- 𠃊 _yǐn_ /hide/ (oneself; become invisible/formless)
- 八 _bā_ /eight/ (trigrams)
/Transcendent Daoist immortal/ >  /absolute sincerity/honesty/ > /pure/ > /original/innate/natural/ > /true/real/authentic/

季旭昇 gives a list of suggestions for where this character may have come from, including it being a simplification of 鼎 (see p664).
I think it is  clear that the original character was  𠤎 + 貝, then perhaps some kind of stand. Preparing shellfish (for eating)?
* 貝
** etymology
Originally a depiction of a spiky shellfish with claws/legs coming out at the bottom. Ancient character that gradually stylised into the modern form. Simplified form 贝 is an ancient simplification. Appears as a semantic component in words related to ‘treasure/money’ because strings of cowrie shells in ancient China were used as jewelry, traded, and served as money. Shells were replaced by coins in the Qin dynasty 
* 赚
The character 賺 _zhuàn_ /buy low, sell high/ combines 貝 _bèi_ /money/, and 兼 _jiān_ /thrifty/ (phonosemantic)
賺 _zuàn_ is a relatively late reading (no fanqie for this in the Han dynasty Shuowen), and the readings are unusual for characters with 兼 (usually jian/lian)
* 安
* 荆
Note that in the simplified version of this character, the 艹 only covers the left side of the character. This is a rare variant of the full form. The full-form character 荊 reflects the traditional structure of the Seal form, with 艹 over 刑 (not 茾+刂, as in the simple form).
It appears that the existence of these two characters as separate unicode points is a mistake, and they should have been treated as one character.
